Forensic Microbiomics, Thanatometagenomics, and Microbial Trace Evidence: Mathematical, Ecological, and Evaluative Frameworks for Forensic StandardizationThe interface between high-throughput metagenomic sequencing, microbial community ecology, and forensic genetic evidence evaluation represents a significant frontier in modern forensic biocomputation. High-throughput sequencing of phylogenetically informative markers and whole metagenomic DNA allows forensic laboratories to analyze microbial communities associated with human remains, physical evidentiary substrates, and crime scenes.Forensic microbiomics operates across three analytical domains:Thanatometagenomics and Post-Mortem Interval (PMI) Estimation: Utilizing the successional turnover of decomposer communities across somatic tissues, epinecrotic margins, and underlying soils to estimate the time elapsed since somatic death.Trace Association and Microbial Individualization: Extracting resident commensal single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and accessory genomic repertoires from contact substrates, weapons, and touch traces to associate individuals with evidentiary items.Forensic Provenancing and Niche Attribution: Profiling specialized anatomical and biogeographical microbial fingerprints to differentiate mixed or degraded body fluids, infer post-mortem relocations, and establish geolocation linkages.The transition of microbial ecology from descriptive research into accredited forensic casework requires standardizing laboratory processes, bioinformatic workflows, and reporting methods. Evidential defensibility requires transitioning away from uncalibrated point-estimate machine learning heuristics toward formal Bayesian Likelihood Ratio (LR) architectures evaluated under European Network of Forensic Science Institutes (ENFSI) and International Society for Forensic Genetics (ISFG) guidelines.

### 2.1 Formalization of NomenclatureForensic taphonomy has historically suffered from overlapping and non-standardized terminology regarding decomposing biological systems. To ensure scientific rigor and evidential consistency, the following definitions must be applied:Thanatomicrobiome: The totality of microbial communities (bacterial, archaeal, viral, and micro-eukaryotic) inhabiting internal organ systems, somatic tissues, and vascular spaces that are nominally sterile in a living host. This includes the liver, spleen, heart, lungs, kidneys, brain, bone marrow, and systemic vasculature following the cessation of somatic circulation.Thanatometagenome: The aggregate genetic material, encompassing functional coding sequences, accessory plasmids, structural RNA genes, and mobilomes, recovered from the thanatomicrobiome through shotgun metagenomic sequencing.Post-Mortem Microbiome: A broad operational umbrella term designating all microbial assemblages present within and upon a deceased organism throughout the post-mortem timeline.Post-Mortem Microbial Succession: The deterministic, temporally structured turnover of microbial community membership, richness, and metabolic activity driven by microenvironmental physicochemical shifts during carcass decomposition.Necrobiome: The multi-trophic biotic network comprising bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists, metazoans (insects, nematodes), and mammalian scavengers interacting with decaying organic biomass.Decomposer Community: The functional subset of microorganisms and saprophagous arthropods directly executing the metabolic degradation of lipids, proteins, and complex biopolymers derived from necrotic tissues.Cadaver Decomposition Island (CDI): The localized, highly concentrated zone of biological, chemical, and edaphic perturbation created in the substrate immediately beneath and surrounding a decomposing cadaver as purge fluids, lipids, and nitrogenous compounds discharge into the soil.Epinecrotic Community: The specialized microbial assemblages colonizing external epidermal and exposed mucosal surfaces (skin, hair, outer oral cavity, conjunctiva) of a decaying corpse.Post-Mortem Microbial Clock: A calibrated, computationally validated mathematical function correlating quantitative metrics of microbial community turnover (ASV abundance vectors, functional gene transcript densities) to the Post-Mortem Interval (PMI) measured in calendar time or thermal units (ADD/ADH).

### 2.4 Fungal Succession and the Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS)Fungal decomposers colonize mammalian remains during advanced decay, utilizing keratin, collagen, and recalcitrant lipids. Soil necrobiome studies demonstrate that while 16S predictive error increases in late decay, ITS profiles maintain structured succession dominated by saprophytic taxa (Candida, Mucor, Penicillium, and Yarrowia). When Mason et al. evaluated random forest regression across human decomposition soils, integrating environmental metrics with ITS features reduced Mean Absolute Error (MAE) across all tested taxonomic tiers, whereas 16S benefits were restricted to higher ranks.

## 3. Microbial Succession During DecompositionMammalian decomposition represents an ecological disturbance cascade that triggers structured shifts in community structure. Somatic autolysis liberates intracellular nutrients, which endogenous and exogenous anaerobes ferment into organic acids and volatile compounds.Taphonomic progression follows distinct chronological phases:Fresh Stage (0 to 24–48 hours): Characterized by somatic autolysis, rapid oxygen depletion, and commensal quiescence.Bloat Stage (2 to 6 days): Intravascular gas accumulation causes abdominal distension, venous marbling, and rapid expansion of colonic anaerobes.Active Decay (5 to 12 days): Purge fluid eruption and structural collapse open the body cavity, leading to rapid colonisation by Dipteran larvae and aerobic decomposers.Advanced Decay (10 to 25+ days): Reduction of soft tissue biomass and larval migration, accompanied by saprophytic fungal growth and significant nitrogen flux into the CDI.During this process, dominant taxa transition systematically: aerotolerant commensals (Streptococcus, Cutibacterium) are superseded by strictly anaerobic enteric bacteria (Clostridium, Bacteroides), which then yield to insect-vectored opportunists (Ignatzschineria, Wohlfahrtiimonas) and late saprophytes (Psychrobacter, Pseudomonas, and fungi).

### 3.2 The Conserved Decomposer NetworkIn a cross-location human taphonomy study, Burcham, Belk et al. tracked 36 terrestrial human cadavers across two distinct climate regimes (temperate deciduous forest versus semi-arid steppe) and varying seasons. The authors demonstrated that despite differences in native soil types and macroclimatic conditions, a conserved interdomain decomposer network of approximately 20 primary microbial taxa consistently assembled during human decomposition.These taxa are rare or undetectable in non-decomposition soils and healthy living human microbiomes. They are vectored to decomposing remains via necrophagous insects (such as Calliphoridae blowflies and Silphidae carrion beetles), establishing a predictable cross-feeding metabolic network specialized in metabolizing protein and lipid degradation products.

## 4. Mathematical Formulations for Post-Mortem Interval ModelingMicrobial succession during decomposition is thermodynamically linked to thermal energy exposure over time. Linear metrics based solely on chronological time ($t$) fail across variable environmental settings.

### 4.1 Accumulated Degree Days (ADD) and Hours (ADH)Accumulated Degree Days ($\text{ADD}$) calculate thermal energy as the integral of ambient temperature above a base physiological threshold ($T_{\text{base}}$):$$\text{ADD} = \sum_{d=1}^{n} \max\left(0, \overline{T}_d - T_{\text{base}}\right)$$where $\overline{T}_d = \frac{T_{d,\max} + T_{d,\min}}{2}$ represents daily mean temperature [Category C]. When hourly logging sensors are present, Accumulated Degree Hours ($\text{ADH}$) provide continuous integration:$$\text{ADH} = \sum_{h=1}^{H} \max\left(0, T_h - T_{\text{base}}\right)$$$$\text{ADD} = \frac{\text{ADH}}{24}$$In forensic entomology and taphonomy, $T_{\text{base}} = 0^\circ\text{C}$ is widely used as a methodological default [Category C]. However, psychrotrophic decomposers (such as Psychrobacter and Carnobacterium) remain metabolically active at sub-zero temperatures (down to $-4^\circ\text{C}$) [Category A]. Conversely, mesophilic enteric taxa decline below $T_{\text{base}} \approx 4\text{--}6^\circ\text{C}$ [Category D]. Thus, assuming a universal $T_{\text{base}} = 0^\circ\text{C}$ is a modeling convention rather than a strict biological constant.

### 4.2 Non-Linear Microbial Temperature KineticsMicrobial growth rates follow non-linear thermal performance curves. The Arrhenius formulation models reaction kinetics across moderate temperature ranges:$$k(T) = A \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{E_a}{R \cdot T}\right)$$where:$k(T)$ is the temperature-dependent rate constant ($\text{time}^{-1}$) [Category C].$A$ is the pre-exponential frequency factor ($\text{time}^{-1}$) [Category D].$E_a$ is the activation energy of the rate-limiting metabolic step ($\text{J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) [Category D].$R$ is the universal gas constant ($8.314462618\text{ J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}$) [Category A].$T$ is absolute temperature ($\text{Kelvin}$).The Arrhenius model assumes that $E_a$ remains constant and disregards high-temperature enzyme denaturation and low-temperature membrane phase transitions [Category C]. For modeling across broader ranges, the Ratkowsky Square-Root Model provides greater empirical fidelity [Category C]:$$\sqrt{k(T)} = b \cdot (T - T_{\min}) \cdot \left(1 - \exp\left(c \cdot (T - T_{\max})\right)\right)$$where $b$ and $c$ are regression coefficients, and $T_{\min}$ and $T_{\max}$ are the physiological growth temperature limits of the microbial community [Category D].

### 4.3 Ecological Modeling FormulationsTemporal turnover within decomposing communities can be modeled using several dynamical frameworks:Generalized Lotka-Volterra (gLV) SystemTracks taxon-taxon interactions within the decomposition matrix:$$\frac{dx_i(t)}{dt} = x_i(t) \left( r_i + \sum_{j=1}^{S} \alpha_{ij} x_j(t) \right)$$where $x_i(t)$ represents the abundance of taxon $i$, $r_i$ is its intrinsic growth rate, and $\alpha_{ij}$ is the interaction coefficient describing the effect of taxon $j$ on taxon $i$ [Category C]. Estimating parameters in high-dimensional settings ($S \gg 100$) requires $\ell_1$-regularization or Bayesian shrinkage priors to prevent overfitting [Category C].Bayesian Hierarchical State-Space ModelSeparates observational noise from underlying ecological succession:$$y_t \sim \text{Dirichlet-Multinomial}(\mathbf{x}_t, \phi)$$$$\mathbf{x}_t = f(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \mathbf{\Theta}) + \mathbf{w}_t, \quad \mathbf{w}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{\Sigma}_w)$$where $y_t$ is the observed sequencing read count vector at time $t$, $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the true underlying compositional state, $\phi$ is an overdispersion parameter, $\mathbf{\Theta}$ represents thermal kinetic parameters, and $\mathbf{\Sigma}_w$ models environmental process variance [Category C].

## 5. Machine Learning Architectures for Post-Mortem Interval RegressionSupervised machine learning algorithms convert high-dimensional compositional features into continuous PMI predictions. The analytical progression follows a structured path: raw count matrices undergo Bayesian zero replacement, are transformed into unconstrained coordinates via CLR or ILR, and are modeled using regularized ensembles. Generalization is evaluated using Leave-One-Subject-Out cross-validation, out-of-domain guardrails, and conformal prediction bands before Likelihood Ratios are generated for casework.

### 5.1 Random Forest RegressionRandom Forest constructs ensembles of de-correlated regression trees grown via bootstrap aggregation (bagging). For $B$ trees $\{T_b\}_{b=1}^B$, the predicted PMI ($\hat{y}$) for a microbial feature vector $\mathbf{x}$ is:$$\hat{y}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} T_b(\mathbf{x})$$At each split within a tree, a subset of $m_{\text{try}}$ features is selected from total features $p$ (methodological default for regression: $m_{\text{try}} = \lfloor p/3 \rfloor$ or $\lfloor \sqrt{p} \rfloor$) [Category C]. Splits are determined by minimizing node impurity (Mean Squared Error):$$\Delta I = \text{MSE}_{\text{parent}} - \left(\frac{N_{\text{left}}}{N} \text{MSE}_{\text{left}} + \frac{N_{\text{right}}}{N} \text{MSE}_{\text{right}}\right)$$Feature Importance Pitfall: Standard Mean Decrease Impurity (Gini importance) artificially overestimates the significance of continuous, correlated, or high-cardinality features [Category C]. Permutation Feature Importance evaluated on Out-Of-Bag (OOB) samples provides an unbiased assessment of feature contributions across decomposition profiles.

### 5.2 Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost)Gradient boosting models iteratively minimize an objective function by fitting new base learners to pseudo-residuals:$$\mathcal{L}^{(t)} = \sum_{i=1}^n l\left(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} + f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)\right) + \Omega(f_t)$$where $\Omega(f_t) = \gamma T_L + \frac{1}{2} \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T_L} w_j^2$ penalizes tree complexity ($T_L$ leaves) and leaf weights ($w$) [Category C]. Second-order Taylor expansion approximations implemented in XGBoost accelerate convergence across sparse metagenomic feature tables.

### 5.3 Regularized Elastic Net RegressionElastic Net combines $\ell_1$ (Lasso) and $\ell_2$ (Ridge) penalties to handle high-dimensional collinearity:$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left[ \frac{1}{2n} \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda \left( \alpha \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_1 + \frac{1-\alpha}{2} \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 \right) \right]$$where:$\lambda \ge 0$ is the global regularization complexity parameter [Category D].$\alpha \in [0, 1]$ balances feature selection ($\alpha=1$, Lasso) and collinear grouping retention ($\alpha=0$, Ridge) [Category C].Elastic Net identifies sparse, interpretable sets of indicator taxa while preserving groups of co-occurring, cross-feeding microbes in the decomposition matrix.

## 6. Compositional Data Analysis (CoDa) in Forensic MetagenomicsHigh-throughput sequencing measures relative abundances rather than absolute cell counts, as the total read count is constrained by library size and sequencer capacity.$$\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_D) \in \mathbb{S}^D \iff x_i > 0, \quad \sum_{i=1}^D x_i = 1$$Applying standard statistical methods (such as Pearson correlations or Euclidean distances) directly to raw compositional proportions generates spurious negative correlations, artifactual co-occurrence networks, and coordinate-dependent distortions. Log-ratio transformations (CLR and ILR) map constrained simplex vectors into unconstrained Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{D-1}$, enabling valid multivariate distance calculations and statistical modeling.

### 6.1 Centered Log-Ratio (CLR) TransformationThe CLR transformation maps composition vectors from the simplex $\mathbb{S}^D$ to an unconstrained Euclidean hyperplane in $\mathbb{R}^D$:$$\text{CLR}(\mathbf{x}) = \left[ \ln\left(\frac{x_1}{g(\mathbf{x})}\right), \ln\left(\frac{x_2}{g(\mathbf{x})}\right), \dots, \ln\left(\frac{x_D}{g(\mathbf{x})}\right) \right]$$where $g(\mathbf{x})$ is the geometric mean of the compositional abundance vector:$$g(\mathbf{x}) = \left( \prod_{j=1}^D x_j \right)^{1/D} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{D} \sum_{j=1}^D \ln x_j \right)$$CLR preserves sub-compositional geometry but produces a singular covariance matrix of rank $D-1$, as $\sum_{i=1}^D \text{CLR}(x_i) = 0$.

### 6.2 Isometric Log-Ratio (ILR) TransformationThe ILR transformation projects compositions onto an orthonormal basis of dimension $D-1$ via a Sequential Binary Partition (SBP):$$\mathbf{y} = \text{ILR}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^* \mathbf{\Psi}^T \in \mathbb{R}^{D-1}$$The $k$-th coordinate of the ILR basis is calculated as:$$y_k = \sqrt{\frac{r_k s_k}{r_k + s_k}} \ln\left( \frac{\left( \prod_{i \in I_+} x_i \right)^{1/r_k}}{\left( \prod_{j \in I_-} x_j \right)^{1/s_k}} \right)$$where $r_k$ and $s_k$ denote the number of taxa in the positive ($I_+$) and negative ($I_-$) partition sets for balance $k$. ILR eliminates singular covariance issues, allowing the application of unconstrained multivariate models (e.g., LDA, QDA, Mahalanobis distance).

### 9.1 The Multi-Compartment Deposition ModelMicrobial transfer during physical contact follows multi-compartment deposition kinetics:$$\text{Person (Skin Reservoir)} \xrightarrow{\eta_1} \text{Hand / Fingertip} \xrightarrow{\eta_2} \text{Evidentiary Substrate} \xrightarrow{\eta_3} \text{Secondary Receiver}$$where $\eta_i$ denotes the transfer efficiency across compartments, which is modulated by surface energy, contact duration, applied friction/pressure, and the hydration state of the epidermal lipid mantle.

### 9.2 Decay Dynamics FormulationsFollowing deposition, transferred microbial cells experience desiccation, nutrient starvation, and UV damage, transitioning through distinct phases of viability loss and DNA degradation.Classical Exponential First-Order Decay$$N(t) = N_0 \cdot \exp(-k_{\text{decay}} \cdot t)$$Assumes a constant inactivation rate ($k_{\text{decay}}$). ## 10. Bayesian Forensic Evidence Evaluation and Likelihood-Ratio FrameworkA microbial similarity metric or classifier output is not inherently a Likelihood Ratio ($\text{LR}$). To be admitted in an evaluative reporting framework, microbial evidence must be evaluated under competing forensic propositions. The analytical process requires modeling within-source distance distributions $f(d \mid H_p)$ and between-source distance distributions $f(d \mid H_d)$ before computing and calibrating the LR via methods such as isotonic regression.

### 10.1 Proposition FramingForensic evaluations must explicitly frame mutually exclusive hypotheses representing prosecution ($H_p$) and defense ($H_d$) positions:$$\begin{aligned}
H_p &: \text{The evidentiary microbial trace originated from the person of interest (suspect).} \\
H_d &: \text{The evidentiary microbial trace originated from an unknown, unrelated individual in the relevant population.}
\end{aligned}$$The Likelihood Ratio ($\text{LR}$) evaluates the probability of observing the analytical data ($E$) given these propositions:$$\text{LR} = \frac{P(E \mid H_p, I)}{P(E \mid H_d, I)}$$where $I$ represents the conditioning background case information.

### 10.2 Score-Based Likelihood Ratios via Kernel Density EstimationDirect calculation of feature-based multivariate probability densities in high-dimensional, sparse simplex space ($D > 1000$) is often mathematically ill-conditioned [Category C]. Forensic biocomputation uses Score-Based Likelihood Ratios ($\text{SLR}$) derived from calibrated compositional distance metrics ($d$, such as Aitchison distance $d_A$):$$\text{SLR}(d) = \frac{f(d \mid H_p)}{f(d \mid H_d)}$$The probability density functions $f(d \mid H_p)$ (within-source variability) and $f(d \mid H_d)$ (between-source variability) are modeled non-parametrically using Gaussian Kernel Density Estimation (KDE):$$f(d \mid H) = \frac{1}{n h} \sum_{i=1}^n K\left(\frac{d - d_i}{h}\right) = \frac{1}{n h \sqrt{2\pi}} \sum_{i=1}^n \exp\left(-\frac{(d - d_i)^2}{2h^2}\right)$$Optimal bandwidth selection ($h$) is determined via Sheather-Jones or cross-validated maximum likelihood methods to prevent probability density collapse near boundary values [Category C].

### 10.3 Calibration and the Log-Likelihood Ratio Cost ($C_{\text{llr}}$)Raw likelihood ratios generated from heuristic distance metrics or uncalibrated machine learning classifiers are often uncalibrated and can overstate evidentiary weight. Calibration applies monotonic transformations (e.g., Logistic Regression or Isotonic Regression / Pool-Adjacent-Violators Algorithm) to align output scores with empirical probabilities.System performance and calibration quality are assessed using the Log-Likelihood Ratio Cost ($C_{\text{llr}}$), formalized by Brümmer and du Preez:$$C_{\text{llr}} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{1}{N_p} \sum_{i=1}^{N_p} \log_2\left(1 + \frac{1}{\text{LR}_i^{(p)}}\right) + \frac{1}{N_d} \sum_{j=1}^{N_d} \log_2\left(1 + \text{LR}_j^{(d)}\right) \right]$$where:$\text{LR}_i^{(p)}$ are likelihood ratios computed for known same-source comparisons ($H_p$ true) [Category C].$\text{LR}_j^{(d)}$ are likelihood ratios computed for known different-source comparisons ($H_d$ true) [Category C].$C_{\text{llr}} = 0$ indicates a perfectly discriminating, calibrated system [Category A].$C_{\text{llr}} = 1$ indicates a system with zero informational value (equivalent to base-rate guessing) [Category A].$C_{\text{llr}} > 1$ reveals an actively misleading, counter-calibrated system [Category A].$C_{\text{llr}}$ can be decomposed into discrimination loss ($C_{\text{llr}}^{\min}$) and calibration loss ($C_{\text{llr}}^{\text{cal}}$):$$C_{\text{llr}} = C_{\text{llr}}^{\min} + C_{\text{llr}}^{\text{cal}}$$Tippett plots illustrate system calibration by displaying the empirical cumulative distribution functions of $\log_{10}(\text{LR})$ for known same-source ($H_p$ true) and known different-source ($H_d$ true) pairs. A well-calibrated, discriminating system displays curves separated symmetrically across the neutral line $\log_{10}(\text{LR}) = 0$, with minimal probability mass representing misleading evidence ($H_p$ pairs falling below 0, or $H_d$ pairs exceeding 0).

## 12. Multi-Class Classification ParadigmsForensic classification tasks use generative models (LDA, QDA) that estimate joint densities $P(X, Y)$ and discriminative models (Multinomial Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost) that estimate decision boundaries $P(Y \mid X)$.

### 12.1 Multinomial Logistic Regression (Softmax Regression)For a classification task with $K$ distinct fluid or tissue classes and an input compositional feature vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ (in CLR or ILR coordinates), the posterior class membership probability is:$$P(Y = k \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\exp\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_k^T \mathbf{x} + \beta_{0k}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^K \exp\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_j^T \mathbf{x} + \beta_{0j}\right)}$$where $\sum_{k=1}^K P(Y = k \mid \mathbf{x}) = 1$. To prevent over-parameterization, one class is defined as the baseline pivot ($\boldsymbol{\beta}_K = \mathbf{0}$). Regularization incorporates an Elastic Net penalty into the multinomial loss:$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}) = -\sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^K I(y_i = k) \ln P(Y = k \mid \mathbf{x}_i) + \lambda \sum_{k=1}^K \left( \alpha \|\boldsymbol{\beta}_k\|_1 + \frac{1-\alpha}{2} \|\boldsymbol{\beta}_k\|_2^2 \right)$$12.2 Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA) and Covariance ShrinkageQDA models class-conditional distributions as multivariate normal densities $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \mathbf{\Sigma}_k)$:$$\delta_k(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{1}{2}\ln|\mathbf{\Sigma}_k| - \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^T \mathbf{\Sigma}_k^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k) + \ln \pi_k$$where $\pi_k$ is the prior probability of class $k$. In microbiome studies where the number of features $p$ frequently exceeds class sample sizes $N_k$, $\mathbf{\Sigma}_k$ becomes singular and non-invertible. To resolve this, Ledoit-Wolf or Schäfer-Strimmer shrinkage estimators are applied:$$\mathbf{\Sigma}_k^*(\gamma) = (1 - \gamma)\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_k + \gamma \mathbf{T}_k$$where $\mathbf{T}_k = \frac{\text{Tr}(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_k)}{p} \mathbf{I}_p$ is the identity target matrix, and $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ is the analytically calculated optimal shrinkage intensity [Category C].

## 14. Strain-Level Profiling, Accessory Genomes, and Source AttributionSpecies-level relative abundances are limited in their discriminatory power because unrelated individuals frequently host similar compositions of core commensal taxa (e.g., Cutibacterium acnes, Staphylococcus epidermidis). Forensic individualization requires resolving strain-level genetic variation.Discriminatory resolution follows a tiered structure:Tier 1: Species Abundance Vectors (16S / Metagenomic Genera): Low discriminatory power; primarily identifies tissue type and broad provenance groups.Tier 2: Single Nucleotide Variants (SNVs) & Clade Subtypes: High discriminatory power; includes hidSkinPlex+ markers and C. acnes phylotypes IA, IB, II.Tier 3: Accessory Pan-Genome Variation, Plasmids, & MGEs: Very high discriminatory power; isolates individual-specific gene insertion/deletion profiles.Tier 4: Hypervariable CRISPR Spacers & Phage Integration Arrays: Extreme discriminatory power; captures individualized immune exposure histories over time [Category C].

### 14.1 Genomic Targets for Forensic Strain ProfilingCore-Genome Single Nucleotide Variants (SNVs): Profiling bi-allelic and multi-allelic SNVs across the stable core genome of resident skin commensals (via tools like StrainPhlAn and InStrain) enables high-resolution donor differentiation.Accessory Pan-Genome Variation: Plasmids, prophages, transposons, and genomic islands vary considerably between individual hosts, reflecting differences in antibiotic exposure and environmental contacts.CRISPR Spacer Arrays: The ordered acquisition of bacteriophage-derived spacer sequences within bacterial CRISPR loci provides an individualized record of exposure, functioning as a dynamic biological signature [Category C].

### 14.2 Likelihood Formulations for Strain-Sharing EvidenceUnder a Bayesian framework, the forensic evaluation of an identical or near-identical microbial strain recovered from an evidentiary trace ($E$) and a person of interest ($K$) is formulated as:$$\text{LR}_{\text{strain}} = \frac{P(E \mid H_p, I)}{P(E \mid H_d, I)} = \frac{1 - P_{\text{mutation/drift}}(\Delta t)}{P_{\text{random match}}(S \mid \mathbf{\theta}_{\text{pop}})}$$where:$P_{\text{mutation/drift}}(\Delta t)$ is the probability that the strain undergoes in situ single-nucleotide mutations or structural rearrangements over the time interval $\Delta t$ between deposition and recovery [Category D].$P_{\text{random match}}(S \mid \mathbf{\theta}_{\text{pop}})$ is the random match probability of encountering strain $S$ in an unrelated individual drawn from the relevant background population, parameterized by microbial population structure $\mathbf{\theta}_{\text{pop}}$ [Category D].Microbial populations exhibit horizontal gene transfer, non-Mendelian recombination, and strong clonal population structures [Category C]. Unlike human STR alleles, allele frequencies cannot be modeled assuming Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium or linkage equilibrium [Category C]. Forensic likelihood ratios for strain evidence require empirical population reference databases.

## 17. Machine Learning Validation, Data Leakage, and GeneralizationA common point of failure in forensic bioinformatics is the conflation of exploratory model fits with validated predictive performance. Overfitting and data leakage can lead to overly optimistic classification and regression estimates that fail when applied to independent operational casework.Data leakage presents across three primary vectors:Subject / Donor Leakage: Splitting longitudinal samples from the same donor across training and testing partitions allows models to memorize individual-specific commensal variants.Batch / Extraction Run Leakage: Processing casework and control groups in separate extraction batches causes classifiers to learn reagent kitome profiles rather than true biological distinctions.Temporal / Autocorrelation Leakage: Random cross-validation across time-series samples from a single cadaver artificially inflates apparent PMI estimation accuracy.

### 17.1 Cross-Validation ArchitecturesTo prevent data leakage, cross-validation architectures must separate biological donors:Standard $k$-Fold Cross-Validation: Inappropriate when multiple time points or body sites originate from the same donor. Random sample splitting distributes donor-specific genomic features across both training and testing subsets, causing donor leakage [Category C].Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) / Leave-One-Donor-Out (LODO): All longitudinal, multi-site samples originating from a single donor must be restricted entirely to either the training or the testing fold.Nested Cross-Validation: Feature selection, zero imputation, and hyperparameter tuning must be executed strictly within inner cross-validation loops, with model generalizability evaluated on an unexposed outer test partition [Category C].

### 17.2 Statistical Performance Metrics for Evaluative SystemsMatthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC): Measures binary/multi-class classification performance across imbalanced datasets:$$\text{MCC} = \frac{\text{TP} \times \text{TN} - \text{FP} \times \text{FN}}{\sqrt{(\text{TP}+\text{FP})(\text{TP}+\text{FN})(\text{TN}+\text{FP})(\text{TN}+\text{FN})}}$$Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) for continuous PMI estimation:$$\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n |y_i - \hat{y}_i|, \quad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}$$Brier Score for multi-class probability calibration:$$\text{BS} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{k=1}^K (P(Y_i = k \mid \mathbf{x}_i) - y_{ik}^*)^2$$where $y_{ik}^* = 1$ if the true class of sample $i$ is $k$, and $0$ otherwise [Category C].

### 18.1 Bioinformatic Decontamination: The decontam FrameworkThe open-source R package decontam identifies and filters contaminant sequences using statistical models [Category C]:Frequency Model: The observed sequence abundance of a contaminant taxon is inversely proportional to the initial total input sample DNA concentration ($C$):$$P(\text{Contaminant} \mid \text{Abundance}, C) \propto \frac{1}{C}$$Prevalence Model: Evaluates the presence/absence ratio of sequences between low-biomass casework specimens and negative control blanks (Extraction Blanks and PCR No-Template Controls) using Fisher's exact test:$$\text{Fisher's } p\text{-value} \le \alpha_{\text{threshold}} \implies \text{Classified as Contaminant \& Removed}$$Typical forensic implementations set $\alpha_{\text{threshold}} = 0.1\text{--}0.5$ to reduce contaminant carryover in trace evidence workflows [Category C].

### 22.1 Python Implementation: CoDa Transformations, Random Forest Regression with LOSO-CV, and Calibrated Likelihood-Ratio EstimationPython"""
Forensic Microbiome Computational Suite
Implements: CLR Transform, Aitchison Distance, LOSO-CV Random Forest PMI Regression,
Score-Based Likelihood Ratio (SLR) estimation, and Cllr Calibration Evaluation.
"""

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import gaussian_kde
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error
from sklearn.model_selection import LeaveOneGroupOut

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 1. COMPOSITIONAL DATA ANALYSIS (CoDa) UTILITIES
# ---------------------------------------------------------

def bayesian_multiplicative_replacement(matrix: np.ndarray, delta: float = 1e-4) -> np.ndarray:
    """
    Implements simple multiplicative replacement for zero counts.
    Ensures relative ratio preservation on the simplex.
    """
    mat = matrix.astype(float)
    zeros = (mat == 0)
    if not np.any(zeros):
        return mat / mat.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    
    # Scale non-zeros down proportionally
    total_zeros = zeros.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    scale_factor = 1.0 - (total_zeros * delta)
    mat_adj = np.where(zeros, delta, mat * scale_factor)
    return mat_adj / mat_adj.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def centered_log_ratio(matrix: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """
    Applies the Centered Log-Ratio (CLR) transformation.
    """
    comp = bayesian_multiplicative_replacement(matrix)
    gm = np.exp(np.mean(np.log(comp), axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return np.log(comp / gm)

def aitchison_distance(u: np.ndarray, v: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """
    Computes Aitchison Distance between two compositional vectors.
    """
    clr_u = centered_log_ratio(u.reshape(1, -1))
    clr_v = centered_log_ratio(v.reshape(1, -1))
    return float(np.linalg.norm(clr_u - clr_v))

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 2. RANDOM FOREST PMI REGRESSION (LOSO CROSS-VALIDATION)
# ---------------------------------------------------------

def evaluate_forensic_pmi_loso(
    X_counts: np.ndarray,
    pmi_add: np.ndarray,
    donor_groups: np.ndarray,
    random_seed: int = 42
) -> dict:
    """
    Executes Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) Cross-Validation to eliminate donor leakage.
    """
    # Transform compositional feature table
    X_clr = centered_log_ratio(X_counts)
    
    logo = LeaveOneGroupOut()
    y_true_all = []
    y_pred_all = []
    
    for train_idx, test_idx in logo.split(X_clr, pmi_add, groups=donor_groups):
        X_train, X_test = X_clr[train_idx], X_clr[test_idx]
        y_train, y_test = pmi_add[train_idx], pmi_add[test_idx]
        
        rf = RandomForestRegressor(
            n_estimators=500,
            max_features="sqrt",
            min_samples_leaf=2,
            random_state=random_seed,
            n_jobs=-1
        )
        rf.fit(X_train, y_train)
        preds = rf.predict(X_test)
        
        y_true_all.extend(y_test)
        y_pred_all.extend(preds)
        
    y_true_all = np.array(y_true_all)
    y_pred_all = np.array(y_pred_all)
    
    mae = mean_absolute_error(y_true_all, y_pred_all)
    rmse = np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y_true_all, y_pred_all))
    
    return {
        "MAE": float(mae),
        "RMSE": float(rmse),
        "y_true": y_true_all,
        "y_pred": y_pred_all
    }

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 3. SCORE-BASED LIKELIHOOD RATIO & Cllr COMPUTATION
# ---------------------------------------------------------

def compute_cllr(lrs_hp: np.ndarray, lrs_hd: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """
    Calculates the Log-Likelihood Ratio Cost (Cllr) for an evaluative system.
    """
    n_hp = len(lrs_hp)
    n_hd = len(lrs_hd)
    
    # Avoid zero division or log domain errors
    hp_term = np.sum(np.log2(1.0 + (1.0 / np.maximum(lrs_hp, 1e-15))))
    hd_term = np.sum(np.log2(1.0 + np.maximum(lrs_hd, 1e-15)))
    
    cllr = (1.0 / (2.0)) * ((1.0 / n_hp) * hp_term + (1.0 / n_hd) * hd_term)
    return float(cllr)

def fit_and_evaluate_score_lr(
    within_source_dists: np.ndarray,
    between_source_dists: np.ndarray,
    unknown_distance: float
) -> dict:
    """
    Fits KDE distributions over distance metrics and computes LR for unknown comparison.
    """
    kde_hp = gaussian_kde(within_source_dists, bw_method="silverman")
    kde_hd = gaussian_kde(between_source_dists, bw_method="silverman")
    
    density_hp = float(kde_hp(unknown_distance))
    density_hd = float(kde_hd(unknown_distance))
    
    # Floor densities to mitigate extreme edge inflation
    density_hp = max(density_hp, 1e-12)
    density_hd = max(density_hd, 1e-12)
    
    lr = density_hp / density_hd
    log10_lr = np.log10(lr)
    
    # Internal evaluation vectors for validation
    lrs_hp = kde_hp(within_source_dists) / np.maximum(kde_hd(within_source_dists), 1e-12)
    lrs_hd = kde_hp(between_source_dists) / np.maximum(kde_hd(between_source_dists), 1e-12)
    system_cllr = compute_cllr(lrs_hp, lrs_hd)
    
    return {
        "Trace_Distance": unknown_distance,
        "Density_Hp": density_hp,
        "Density_Hd": density_hd,
        "Likelihood_Ratio": lr,
        "Log10_LR": log10_lr,
        "System_Cllr": system_cllr
    }

## 24. Forensic Microbiome Statistical Failure AnalysisDeploying microbial evidence in legal proceedings requires identifying potential methodological and statistical pitfalls:Compositionality Ignorance: Treating relative abundance data as unconstrained Euclidean vectors generates spurious correlations and coordinate-dependent distortions. All quantitative models must operate in log-ratio coordinates (CLR, ILR) or use compositionally robust metrics.Subject and Household Pseudoreplication: Treating multiple samples collected longitudinally from the same individual or decomposing body as independent observations inflates effective degrees of freedom. Grouped cross-validation (Leave-One-Donor-Out) is required to evaluate generalizability.The Extraction Kitome Confounder: Failing to co-sequence negative controls (Extraction Blanks and No-Template Controls) alongside low-biomass forensic traces can lead to reagent contaminants being misinterpreted as probative evidentiary markers.$p$-Hacking Across Hypervariable Regions and Taxonomic Ranks: Iterating through multiple 16S primer regions (V1-V2, V3-V4, V4) and taxonomic levels without adjusting for multiple testing (e.g., Benjamini-Hochberg FDR) inflates false discovery rates [Category C].Survivor and Dropout Bias in Advanced Taphonomy: In late-stage decomposition, soft tissue lysis can reduce endogenous bacterial biomass below assay limits. ## 25. Statistical Power Analysis and Experimental SizingForensic validation requires designing studies with adequate statistical power to detect meaningful biological differences and calibrate evaluative models. Experimental sizing must satisfy several statistical conditions:Multivariate Community Separation (PERMANOVA Power): Achieving power $\ge 0.80$ at $\alpha = 0.05$ with moderate effect size ($\omega^2 \ge 0.15$) requires $n \ge 8\text{--}12$ independent donors per taphonomic cohort.Machine Learning Generalization (LOSO-CV PMI Stability): Minimum of $n \ge 30\text{--}50$ human donors across multi-climate longitudinal taphonomy series.Score-Based LR Calibration (ISO 17025 / ENFSI Validation): Requires $N_p \ge 100\text{--}200$ known same-source and $N_d \ge 1000\text{--}10,000$ known different-source validation pairs.Rare-Event False Inclusion Bounds (Rule of Three): Demonstrating a false match probability $<0.1\%$ requires at least 3,000 zero-error non-matching comparisons [Category C].

### 25.1 PERMANOVA Power Estimation for Community DivergencePermutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) evaluates compositional differences between experimental cohorts. Statistical power is a function of sample size ($N$), within-group multivariate dispersion, and effect size ($\omega^2$):$$\omega^2 = \frac{\text{SS}_{\text{between}} - (k - 1)\text{MS}_{\text{within}}}{\text{SS}_{\text{total}} + \text{MS}_{\text{within}}}$$For complex decomposition studies with moderate dispersion, achieving statistical power of $(1 - \beta) \ge 0.80$ at $\alpha = 0.05$ typically requires a minimum sample size of $n = 8\text{--}12$ distinct human donors per experimental condition or taphonomic factor [Category C].

### 25.2 Sample Size Requirements for Score-Based LR CalibrationValidating forensic Likelihood Ratio models in accordance with ISO/IEC 17025 and ENFSI recommendations requires sufficient comparison pairs to estimate tail probabilities:Known Same-Source Pairs ($H_p$): $N_p \ge 100\text{--}200$ comparisons across varied post-deposition time intervals and substrates [Category C].Known Different-Source Pairs ($H_d$): $N_d \ge 1,000\text{--}10,000$ non-matching comparisons to evaluate the likelihood of rare coincidental matches in the background population [Category C].

### 25.3 Rare Event False Inclusion Bounds (The Rule of Three)If zero false inclusions are observed across $N$ independent, non-matching validation comparisons, the upper $95\%$ confidence limit of the false match probability ($p_{\text{error}}$) is calculated as:$$p_{\text{upper 95\%}} \approx \frac{3}{N}$$For instance, demonstrating that the false positive match rate of a touch microbiome panel is $<0.1\%$ ($p < 0.001$) at a $95\%$ confidence level requires a minimum of $N = 3,000$ known different-source validation tests with zero observed false matches [Category C].

## 26. Measurement Uncertainty and Error PropagationAll quantitative outputs derived from forensic microbiomics pipelines must be reported with associated measurement uncertainty.

### 26.1 Expanded Uncertainty in Post-Mortem Interval EstimationUnder the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM), the expanded uncertainty ($U$) for an estimated post-mortem interval is:$$U = k_{\text{coverage}} \cdot u_c(\hat{y})$$where $u_c(\hat{y})$ is the combined standard uncertainty, and $k_{\text{coverage}}$ is the coverage factor [Category C]. When the error distribution is Gaussian and the degrees of freedom are high ($df > 30$), choosing $k_{\text{coverage}} = 2.00$ provides an approximate $95.45\%$ coverage interval [Category C].Microbiome-based PMI prediction errors are often non-Gaussian and heteroscedastic, displaying wider variance during advanced decomposition stages. Assuming $k_{\text{coverage}} = 2.00$ without empirical verification can misrepresent the uncertainty interval.

### 26.2 Conformal Prediction IntervalsTo provide non-parametric, finite-sample prediction intervals without assuming Gaussian error distributions, forensic regression pipelines can implement Inductive Conformal Prediction (ICP) [Category C]:Partition calibration data into a training set and a separate calibration set $(i = 1, \dots, m)$.Fit the machine learning model $\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{x})$ on the training subset.Compute non-conformity scores on calibration samples: $\alpha_i = |y_i - \hat{\mu}(\mathbf{x}_i)|$.For a given significance level $\epsilon = 0.05$ (yielding $95\%$ coverage), calculate the $(1 - \epsilon)(1 + 1/m)$-th empirical quantile ($\hat{q}$) of non-conformity scores.Construct the prediction band for an unknown casework profile $\mathbf{x}_{\text{evidentiary}}$:$$\Gamma^{0.95}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{evidentiary}}) = \left[ \hat{\mu}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{evidentiary}}) - \hat{q}, \quad \hat{\mu}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{evidentiary}}) + \hat{q} \right]$$This guarantees valid coverage under the single operational assumption that calibration samples and casework profiles are exchangeable [Category C].

## 27. Bayesian Evidence Theory and Logical Fallacies in Forensic MicrobiologyForensic evaluative reporting requires a rigorous probabilistic foundation to prevent misinterpreting microbial associations in court. The mathematical relationship between prior beliefs, scientific evidence, and posterior probabilities is governed by Bayes' Theorem in odds form:$$\frac{P(H_p \mid E, I)}{P(H_d \mid E, I)} = \frac{P(E \mid H_p, I)}{P(E \mid H_d, I)} \times \frac{P(H_p \mid I)}{P(H_d \mid I)}$$$$\text{Posterior Odds} = \text{Likelihood Ratio (LR)} \times \text{Prior Odds}$$27.1 Common Interpretative FallaciesThe Transposed Conditional (Prosecutor's Fallacy): Conflating the likelihood of the evidence given a hypothesis, $P(E \mid H_d)$, with the probability of the hypothesis given the evidence, $P(H_d \mid E)$.Incorrect Statement: "The probability that this skin trace originated from someone other than the suspect is 1 in 10,000."Correct Statement: "The observed microbial profile is 10,000 times more likely to occur if the trace originated from the suspect than if it originated from an unknown, unrelated individual."The Defense Attorney's Fallacy: Assuming that because other individuals in a population could share common commensal strains, the evidence has no discriminatory value, thereby ignoring the magnitude of the Likelihood Ratio.The Association-Equals-Source Fallacy: Inferring source identity from community similarity metrics alone, without accounting for background population allele frequencies and transfer dynamics.

## 40. Contradictions, Negative Findings, and Methodological InconsistenciesForensic microbiomics literature contains several contradictory findings that highlight the sensitivity of microbial systems to external variables:Universal Decomposer Networks vs. Edaphic Specificity: Burcham, Belk et al. demonstrated a conserved decomposer network assembling across divergent climate types. In contrast, Mason et al. observed that soil-based regression models exhibited substantial error when generalized across broader donor populations in variable edaphic settings. This discrepancy stems primarily from sampling locus: epinecrotic swabs and insect-associated communities reflect universal carrion decomposers, whereas bulk soil samples are strongly influenced by local mineralogy, native baseline ecology, and soil moisture variations.Touch Individualization vs. Transient Environmental Shedding: Controlled studies using targeted SNP panels (e.g., hidSkinPlex+) report host attribution accuracies up to $95\%$ within closed cohorts. Conversely, realistic mock crime scene evaluations show identification rates dropping to $20\%\text{--}25\%$. This performance gap is driven by low biomass recovery, transient bacterial shedding from touched objects, secondary transfer between cohabiting individuals, and post-deposition decay on exposed substrates.Cross-Laboratory Bioinformatic Sensitivity: Identical raw sequencing FASTQ files processed through different denoising algorithms (DADA2 ASVs vs $97\%$ OTU clustering) or against differing database releases (SILVA vs GTDB) can yield divergent downstream taxonomic tables and alter calculated Likelihood Ratios.
